## An unusually candid methodological aside

Most textbooks simply *assume* profit maximization and move on; Osborne & Rubinstein instead pause to ask why, and decline to settle the question. They list, without endorsing any one, four possible justifications economists give: **normative** (producers *should* maximize profit — a claim about what's good, not what happens); **descriptive** (producers, as an empirical matter, mostly *do*); **methodological** (profit-maximization is assumed because it's the assumption that yields analytically tractable, non-trivial results — a modeling convenience elevated to a behavioral claim); and **pedagogical**, more cynically — students are taught it so they come to *believe* it's the only legitimate goal, regardless of whether that belief is warranted.

This is worth flagging explicitly against [the parallel treatment of consumer preferences](../consumer-theory/00-overview.md): consumer theory is built on comparatively mild structural assumptions (completeness, transitivity, and *optionally* monotonicity/convexity/differentiability, motivated and tested individually), while producer theory, in most treatments, simply picks *one specific objective function* (profit) without the same scrutiny — an asymmetry in how much justification the two branches of the theory are typically given, made visible only because this chapter chooses to make it visible.

## The layoff vignette, revisited

Returning to [the opening scenario](the-producer-and-production-functions.md): the profit-maximizing choice is to retain 100 workers (laying off 96, for \$2M profit) — but when the question is actually put to students, **only about half of economics students choose it**, and economics students lay off more workers on average than students of philosophy, law, mathematics, or business. The textbook is careful not to over-interpret this: it could reflect **selection** (people who find profit-maximization congenial are more likely to choose to study economics in the first place) or **indoctrination** (studying material that assumes profit-maximization measurably shifts what students later treat as the natural or correct objective) — the data alone can't distinguish the two, and the authors say so explicitly rather than picking a side.

The broader point, stated as the chapter's closing line: even *within* the discipline that most consistently assumes profit-maximization, a near-majority of its own most advanced students don't unreflectively adopt it once the question is posed concretely — a genuine invitation to treat "the producer maximizes profit" as a modeling choice under active debate, not a settled fact about how firms behave.
