## The other objective, applied to a monopolist

Just as [an output-maximizing producer](../producer-theory/output-vs-profit-maximization.md) produces as much as possible without running a loss, an output-maximizing monopolist produces the output $y^*$ solving $\text{AC}(y^*)=P(y^*)$ — the largest quantity at which price still covers average cost, exactly the [break-even condition from ordinary output-maximization](../producer-theory/cost-functions.md), now applied with $P(y)$ (demand) standing in for the fixed output price of the price-taking case.

## Where output-maximizing monopoly sits relative to the two benchmarks

This gives a genuinely useful third point of comparison, alongside [the profit-maximizing monopoly output $y^*_{\pi}$ (where MR = MC) and the efficient competitive output $y^c$ (where $P=\text{MC}$)](profit-maximizing-monopoly-and-deadweight-loss.md): since average cost sits *below* price over the relevant range whenever marginal cost is below average cost (the usual case for a firm not yet at its efficient scale), the output-maximizing monopolist's output typically lands **between** the restrictive profit-maximizing quantity and the fully efficient one — closer to $y^c$ than a profit-maximizer gets, without fully closing the gap. This is a direct, concrete illustration of the point [raised for producers generally](../producer-theory/why-profit-maximization.md): the objective a firm is assumed to pursue is not a neutral modeling detail — here it visibly changes how much of the standard "monopoly is inefficient" story actually holds.

*Source: Osborne & Rubinstein (2020), Models in Microeconomic Theory, §7.2.2.*
