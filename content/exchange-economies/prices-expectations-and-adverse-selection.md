## A new ingredient: equilibrium beliefs, not just equilibrium prices

Three models sharing a structure beyond ordinary market-clearing: each individual's optimal action depends on a **belief** about something (waiting time, average quality, unit cost) that is itself *generated by everyone else's aggregate behavior* — so equilibrium now requires both optimal actions **and** correct, self-confirming beliefs about that aggregate.

## Model 1: choosing a bank branch (a Hotelling-style location model)

Individuals live along $[0,1]$; branches sit at each end with waiting time $f_j(n_j)$ increasing in the fraction $n_j$ who use branch $j$. Each individual picks the branch minimizing travel-plus-waiting time, given her *belief* about waiting times; equilibrium requires that belief to be correct.

**Proposition.** A unique equilibrium exists: a cutoff point $z^*$ (solving $z^*+f_0(z^*) = (1-z^*)+f_1(1-z^*)$) splits the population, everyone left of $z^*$ using branch 0, everyone right using branch 1 — and this equilibrium is **Pareto stable**. The logic is a self-correcting congestion argument: if more people *tried* to switch to branch 0 than the equilibrium prescribes, its waiting time would rise, making the switch self-defeating — exactly why $z^*$ is the *unique* stable split.

## Model 2: the market for lemons (Akerlof-style adverse selection)

Owners know their own car's quality $Q(i)$; buyers can't observe it and must trade at a single price, valuing any car of believed average quality $\hat q$ at $\alpha\hat q$ ($\alpha>1$, so trade is always potentially gainful). An owner sells iff price exceeds her car's quality; a buyer buys iff price is below $\alpha$ times her *belief* about average quality; equilibrium requires that belief to match the *actual* average quality of cars actually offered.

**Proposition (existence, and its dark side).** An equilibrium with trade always exists, and in *any* trading equilibrium, every car sold has quality no higher than every car **not** sold — sellers of high-quality cars, quite rationally, opt out first.

*Figure — In equilibrium, only the lowest-quality cars sell (black); the price reflects the *average* quality of those cars, which is too low to tempt owners of the better cars (gray) into selling. Under restrictive enough parameters (e.g. $\alpha<4/3$ with evenly-spaced qualities), this unravels all the way down: only the single lowest-quality car trades at all.*

Two further points sharpen the picture: **a no-trade equilibrium also always exists** (if buyers happen to believe average quality is too low to justify any positive price, no one sells, confirming that belief) — though this relies on an unrestricted, arguably unreasonable belief off the equilibrium path. And **any equilibrium with partial trade is not Pareto stable** whenever some unsold car's owner and an unsatisfied buyer could still strike a mutually beneficial private deal at a price between the car's quality and $\alpha$ times its quality — the single uniform market price, not any fundamental scarcity, is what leaves those gains unrealized.

## Model 3: a fishing economy (briefly)

A shared resource (fish) whose extraction cost rises with aggregate catch — each fisher optimizes her own catch taking the (equilibrium-consistent) unit cost as given, in the same "correct aggregate beliefs" spirit as the two models above. The chapter's guiding question — do fishers, each individually rational, collectively over-fish relative to what a coordinated reduction could achieve — previews exactly the externality logic that motivates thinking about common-pool resources and (implicitly) the case for intervention beyond pure price-taking equilibrium.

*Source: Osborne & Rubinstein (2020), Models in Microeconomic Theory, §13. The lemons model is due to Akerlof (1970).*
