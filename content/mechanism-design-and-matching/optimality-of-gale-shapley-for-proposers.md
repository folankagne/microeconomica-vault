## Which side benefits from proposing?

[Stability](stability-and-the-gale-shapley-matching.md) rules out blocking pairs, but a society can have **many** stable matchings simultaneously. $\mathrm{GS}^X$ (the $X$'s propose) finds one, $\mathrm{GS}^Y$ finds another — possibly the same, possibly not, as already flagged in [the worked example](the-gale-shapley-algorithm.md#worked-example). This raises a natural question: does it matter, from an individual's point of view, which side gets to propose? The answer is a clean and striking yes — proposing is (weakly) an advantage for *every* member of the proposing side, and a (weak) disadvantage for every member of the other side, compared to *every* stable matching whatsoever.

**Proposition 18.3.** For any society and preference profile, no stable matching is better for any $X$ than the matching generated by $\mathrm{GS}^X$.

*Proof idea.* Let $\mu$ be the $\mathrm{GS}^X$ matching and suppose, for contradiction, some other stable matching $\psi$ has $\psi(x)\succ_x\mu(x)$ for some $x$. Among all $X$'s for whom this holds, take $x_0$ whose rejection by $y_0:=\psi(x_0)$ (during the $\mathrm{GS}^X$ run) happened **earliest**. Let $x_1$ be the $X$ that $y_0$ chose over $x_0$ at that moment, and let $y_1=\psi(x_1)$.

Since $y_0$ rejected $x_0$ in favor of $x_1$ during the algorithm, $y_0$ prefers $x_1$ to $x_0$. Two cases: if $y_0$ additionally prefers $x_1$ to $y_1$'s partner under $\psi$ — i.e. $x_1$ prefers $y_0$ to $y_1=\psi(x_1)$ — then $(x_1,y_0)$ is a blocking pair for $\psi$, contradicting $\psi$'s stability. So instead $x_1$ must prefer $y_1$ to $y_0$; but then, tracing the $\mathrm{GS}^X$ run, $x_1$ must have proposed to $y_1$ and been rejected *before* the stage at which $y_0$ rejected $x_0$ — meaning $x_1$ is rejected by her $\psi$-partner even earlier than $x_0$ is, contradicting the minimality of $x_0$'s rejection time. Either branch is a contradiction, so no such $x$ exists. $\blacksquare$

So $\mathrm{GS}^X$ delivers, simultaneously for **every single** $x\in X$, the best partner she can get in *any* stable matching — not merely the best matching on average, but literally her most-preferred stable option, for all $X$'s at once. This is a remarkably strong guarantee: a priori there's no reason to expect a single matching to be simultaneously optimal for every member of one side.

## The flip side: proposing is a disadvantage for the receivers

**Proposition 18.4.** For any society and preference profile, no stable matching is worse for any $Y$ than the matching generated by $\mathrm{GS}^X$.

*Proof idea.* Let $\mu$ be the $\mathrm{GS}^X$ matching and suppose some stable $\psi$ is worse for some $y$: $x_2:=\psi^{-1}(y)\prec_y\mu^{-1}(y)=:x_1$, i.e. $y$'s $\psi$-partner is worse for her than her $\mu$-partner $x_1$. Let $y_1=\psi(x_1)$. By Proposition 18.3, $x_1$ (weakly) prefers $\mu(x_1)=y$ to $\psi(x_1)=y_1$, i.e. $y\succsim_{x_1}y_1$. Combined with $x_1\succ_y x_2=\psi^{-1}(y)$ (from $y$ preferring her $\mu$-partner to her $\psi$-partner), $x_1$ and $y$ each prefer each other to their $\psi$-assigned partners — a blocking pair for $\psi$, contradicting its stability. $\blacksquare$

Putting the two propositions together: $\mathrm{GS}^X$ is simultaneously the **$X$-optimal** and **$Y$-pessimal** stable matching. Since $\mathrm{GS}^Y$ is, symmetrically, $Y$-optimal and $X$-pessimal, the two algorithms in general bracket the *entire* range of stable outcomes from each side's point of view — whichever side initiates proposals ends up (weakly) better off than under any alternative stable arrangement, and the other side ends up (weakly) worse off than under any alternative stable arrangement. This gives real economic content to a design choice — who proposes — that might otherwise look like a mere implementation detail: it is exactly the lever that decides which side of the market a matching mechanism systematically favors, echoing how [initial property rights, not raw ability, pin down who benefits under the market mechanism](../equilibrium-foundations/welfare-theorems-for-the-housing-market.md) in the housing-market model.
