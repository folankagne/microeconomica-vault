## Going the other direction: from demand back to preferences

Sections 5.3–5.4 derived demand from a given preference relation. This entry runs the logic in reverse — starting from an *observed* demand function, possibly generated by some rule that isn't utility maximization at all, and asking whether it's *consistent with* maximizing some preference relation.

**Definition — rationalizable demand function.** A demand function is rationalizable if there is a monotone preference relation $\succeq$ such that for every budget set, the bundle the demand function specifies solves the consumer's problem for $\succeq$.

(Unlike [general rationalizability](../preferences-and-choice/choice-functions-and-rationalizability.md), this doesn't require the demanded bundle to be the *unique* solution — ties are allowed, since [budget-set optima are typically boundary points where uniqueness needs strict convexity](the-consumers-problem.md) to guarantee.)

**One demand function, many rationalizing preferences.** "Spend all wealth on the cheaper good" is rationalized by $x_1+x_2$ — but also by $\max\{x_1,x_2\}$ and by $x_1^2+x_2^2$. "Split wealth evenly" is rationalized by $x_1x_2$ — a function that looks like an odd thing to maximize until you see what demand behavior it implies. Rationalizability is a **many-to-one** relationship: recovering "the" preference from demand data is generally underdetermined without further structure.

**A demand function that cannot be rationalized.** "Spend everything on the *more* expensive good": from $B((1,2),2)$, buy bundle $a=(2,0)$; from $B((2,1),2)$, buy $b=(0,2)$. But $b$ sits strictly *inside* $B((1,2),2)$ (it's affordable there with money to spare), so any monotone $\succeq$ rationalizing this demand would need $a\succ b$ (since $b$ is interior and $a$ is chosen) — yet by the symmetric argument on the other budget set, $b\succ a$. Contradiction. This particular demand pattern would make sense if price itself signals quality or confers prestige — exactly the kind of environment where the model's *hidden* assumption (prices carry no information, preferences don't depend on prices or wealth) is violated.

## The weak axiom of revealed preference (WARP)

**Definition — revealed preference.** Bundle $a$ is *revealed at least as good as* $b$ if some budget set contains both and $a$ is chosen. $a$ is *revealed better than* $b$ if some budget set contains both, $a$ is chosen, and $b$ costs strictly less than the budget ($b$ was affordable with room to spare).

**Definition — WARP.** No two bundles $a,b$ can have $a$ revealed at least as good as $b$ *and* $b$ revealed better than $a$.

**Proposition.** Demand rationalized by a monotone preference relation satisfies WARP.

*Proof:* suppose not — $a\succeq b$ (from $a$ revealed at-least-as-good) and, from some other budget set, $b$ chosen with $a$ strictly affordable inside it (revealing $b\succ a$). Take a bundle $c$ in that second budget set with strictly more of both goods than $a$; monotonicity gives $c\succ a$, and since $b$ is chosen from a set containing $c$, $b\succeq c$. Transitivity then forces $b\succ a$, contradicting $a\succeq b$. $\blacksquare$

This gives only a **necessary**, not sufficient, condition — unlike [property $\alpha$'s clean if-and-only-if characterization](../preferences-and-choice/property-alpha-and-the-rationalizability-theorem.md) for general choice functions. [Property $\alpha$ itself is vacuous on the demand-function domain](budget-sets-and-demand-functions.md), which is exactly why WARP had to be introduced as a purpose-built substitute rather than reused directly; a full necessary-and-sufficient characterization exists (the *strong* axiom of revealed preference) but is outside this course's scope.

*Source: Osborne & Rubinstein (2020), Models in Microeconomic Theory, §5.5.*
