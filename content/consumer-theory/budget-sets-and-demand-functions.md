## Budget sets as the consumer's choice problems

Not every subset of $\mathbb{R}^2_+$ is a choice problem a consumer actually faces. The relevant restriction: given prices $p_1,p_2>0$ and wealth $w>0$,

$$B((p_1,p_2),w) = \{(x_1,x_2)\in X : p_1x_1+p_2x_2\leq w\}$$

— a right triangle with legs on the axes, vertices at $(0,0)$, $(w/p_1,0)$, $(0,w/p_2)$. The **budget line** $\{p_1x_1+p_2x_2=w\}$ has slope $-p_1/p_2$: buying one more unit of good 1 costs exactly $p_1/p_2$ units of good 2.

Two structural facts do a lot of later work: (i) $B((\lambda p_1,\lambda p_2),\lambda w)=B((p_1,p_2),w)$ for any $\lambda>0$ — only *relative* prices and wealth matter, so demand is homogeneous of degree zero in $(p_1,p_2,w)$; (ii) every budget set is **convex** — a direct algebraic check on the defining inequality — meaning [convexity of preferences](monotonicity-continuity-and-convexity.md) interacts with a choice environment that is itself always convex, which is exactly what [the uniqueness half of the consumer's problem](the-consumers-problem.md) will exploit.

The same collection of choice problems also arises from a pure-exchange framing: a consumer endowed with bundle $e$, able to trade good 1 for good 2 at rate $\beta$, faces exactly $B((\beta,1),\beta e_1+e_2)$ — prices are not conceptually necessary to generate budget-set choice problems, only a fixed exchange rate is.

## Demand functions, not assumed to come from optimization

**Definition.** A demand function $x$ assigns to every budget set $B((p_1,p_2),w)$ one of its members $x((p_1,p_2),w)$.

Crucially, this definition says nothing about *how* the bundle is selected — it is [a choice function](../preferences-and-choice/choice-functions-and-rationalizability.md) restricted to the specific domain of budget sets, and the chapter deliberately studies non-optimizing behavioral rules alongside utility-maximizing ones:

- **Cheaper good only**: all wealth on whichever good is cheaper (split evenly if tied).
- **Equal quantities**: $x=(w/(p_1+p_2), w/(p_1+p_2))$ regardless of relative prices.
- **Fixed budget shares**: half of wealth on each good, i.e. $(w/(2p_1), w/(2p_2))$.
- **Satiation then spillover**: buy up to 7 units of good 1, spend the rest on good 2.

All four automatically satisfy the zero-degree-homogeneity property inherited from budget sets themselves — a demand function doesn't need to be derived from preference maximization to inherit that particular invariance, since it's a property of the *domain* (budget sets), not of the optimization.

## Why property α says nothing here

[Property $\alpha$](../preferences-and-choice/property-alpha-and-the-rationalizability-theorem.md) characterized rationalizability for general choice functions — but it is **vacuous** for demand functions specifically. If bundle $a$ is chosen from $B$ and sits on $B$'s frontier (as an optimal bundle typically does under monotonicity), no *other* budget set containing $a$ is ever a strict subset of $B$ — budget sets containing a boundary point of $B$ either equal $B$ or extend past it in some direction, since they're generated by a completely different pair $(p,w)$, not by removing elements from $B$. This is why [a dedicated notion — the weak axiom of revealed preference — is needed for demand functions](rationalizing-demand-and-warp.md) rather than reusing property $\alpha$ off the shelf.
